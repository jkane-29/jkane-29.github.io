Woodshop

Working with wood teaches patience and precision in ways that complement the rapid iteration of software development. Each project is a study in constraint, material properties, and the unexpected beauty that emerges from embracing rather than fighting limitations.

[Photo: Walnut desk with dovetail joints]

Standing Desk

Black Walnut • 2024

A height-adjustable desk designed around the constraints of a small home office. The mechanism is hidden within the apron, and the surface adjusts from 29" to 42" using a counterweight system inspired by old drafting tables. The walnut came from a tree that fell in my neighborhood during a storm—there's something satisfying about giving it a second life as furniture.

Hand-cut dovetails, traditional mortise and tenon joinery, finished with Danish oil

[Photo: Cherry bookshelf with through-tenons]

Modular Bookshelf System

Cherry • 2023

A series of interlocking boxes that can be arranged and rearranged as needed. Each unit is identical but can function as a shelf, side table, or room divider depending on configuration. The through-tenons are both structural and decorative— they reveal the construction method while creating visual rhythm across the modules.

Through-tenon construction, hand-planed surfaces, beeswax finish

[Photo: Oak cutting board with geometric pattern]

End-Grain Cutting Boards

White Oak, Maple • 2023

A series of cutting boards exploring geometric patterns that emerge from the systematic arrangement of end-grain pieces. Each board starts with a different mathematical constraint—hexagonal tiling, Fibonacci sequences, or golden ratio proportions—but the final pattern is always influenced by the natural variation in the wood grain.

End-grain construction, food-safe mineral oil finish, brass hanging hardware

[Photo: Bent lamination chair back detail]

Bent Lamination Chair

Ash, Leather • 2022

An experiment in steam bending and lamination techniques. The backrest is formed from thin strips of ash bent around a custom jig, creating a curve that's both structurally sound and comfortable. The seat is hand-stitched leather suspended in a wooden frame—a detail borrowed from traditional Danish furniture but adapted for easier repair and replacement.

Steam-bent laminations, hand-stitched leather seat, shellac finish

[Photo: Japanese-style wooden plane]

Tool Making

Beech, Steel • Ongoing

Making the tools to make the furniture. Started with a simple wooden plane following traditional Japanese techniques, then moved on to chisels, marking gauges, and specialty jigs. There's something deeply satisfying about using tools you've made yourself—it creates an unbroken chain from raw material to finished piece.

Traditional joinery, hand-forged steel, fitted to my specific grip and usage patterns
